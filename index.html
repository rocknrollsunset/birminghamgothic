A Birmingham Gothic Story

Cedar Gap, Alabama. October 1985.

Nineteen-year-old Clay Ashby works the graveyard shift at WCGP, a thousand-watt AM station that used to be a funeral home. At 4:32 one morning, the minute he was born, the request line blinks. Nobody has used that phone since 1979.

A woman’s voice asks for a song he has never heard and tells him where to find it: an unlabeled acetate hidden behind the Lawrence Welk records in the old embalming room. Every time Clay plays it, the lacquer wears thinner. Every time, the song changes.

His father is the preacher leading the town’s Halloween record burning. His grandmother has played the church organ for fifty-four years. And down the hill, under the cedars, something is waiting for somebody to play it backwards.

MON 10·21·85 · 4:32 A

When a call came in, the bulb blinked.

A small red heartbeat in the dark.

I’d worked there for seven months and I had never seen it blink.

At 4:32 that morning, it did.

I picked up for the first time in seven months.

“WCGP, request line.”

For a second there was nothing.

Not a dial tone.

Not breathing.

Something under both of those, like the sound inside a seashell, if the seashell was a long way off and full of rain.

Then a woman said, “Hey there, Claybird.”

Nobody called me that.

Real hauntings, real history

Birmingham was built on iron and ore, and people died building it. These are the hauntings with real history behind them: real names, real dates, real records. When a story was made up, we say so.

Sloss Furnaces

20 32nd Street North

First blast April 1882. Two furnaces, 60 feet tall, turning Red Mountain ore into pig iron. Sloss used convict labor in its coal mines; in 1890, 90 of 1,000 prisoners died in those mines. The one ghost with a real death behind him is Theophilus Calvin Jowers, an assistant foundryman who fell into molten iron in September 1887. The famous ghost, James ‘Slag’ Wormwood, never existed: Sloss’s own director said in 2023 that the story was fabricated to sell tickets to a haunted house.

Alabama Theatre

1817 3rd Avenue North

Previewed Christmas Day, 1927. Paramount called it ‘The Showplace of the South.’ In 1986, house organist Cecil Whitmire and a singer, alone in the theatre, watched a figure cross the stage and vanish. Whitmire was convinced it was his predecessor Stanleigh Malotte, house organist from 1936 to 1955. Two witnesses, a named man, a date. The best documented firsthand apparition account in the city.

Photo by Lee Adlaf, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Tutwiler Hotel

2021 Park Place

Two buildings, one name. The original opened in 1914 and was imploded in 1974. The Tutwiler standing today is the former Ridgely Apartments, a hotel since 1986. Every haunting report belongs to this building. The best known is the Knocker: loud, rapid knocking on sixth floor doors in the early hours, and an empty corridor when the door opens. In November 2004, a woman in Room 604 called the front desk frightened.

Photo by Chris Pruitt, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Arlington Antebellum Home

331 Cotton Avenue SW

The oldest surviving structure in Birmingham. The core is a four room pioneer home from around 1822. In the spring of 1865, Union General James H. Wilson made it his headquarters. Docents have heard doors slam and watched rocking chairs rock on their own. Southern Ghost Girls Tours runs ghost hunts there now.

Photo by Jet Lowe, Historic American Buildings Survey, Library of Congress, public domain

Oak Hill Cemetery

Between 17th and 19th Streets and 11th Avenue and 13th Court North

Birmingham’s first official city cemetery, formally established in December 1871, with more than 10,000 burials. Emma Hawes and her daughters May and Irene, murdered in December 1888, lay in unmarked graves here until a granite marker was placed in 2024. The Erswell vault holds Edward Erswell, a cabinet maker who became a coffin maker in the 1873 cholera epidemic; the legend says whispers can be heard from the tomb late at night, his wife Catherine still complaining about being buried in the wrong place.
